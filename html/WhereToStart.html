Where to Start When You're Worried

If you're here because something about your child has you worried, you're in the right place, and you're not alone. Many parents reach this point without knowing what's out there, which words to search for, or whether what they're seeing is "normal". This page is a short map: what to check first, when to get help, and how learning in Pulse Parenting can help you respond.

1. First, is anything urgent?

Get help now (your local emergency number, or a crisis line) if your child:

  • talks about wanting to die, or about hurting or killing themselves, or you find signs they have hurt themselves;
  • is a danger to someone else;
  • seems confused, sees or hears things others don't, or isn't making sense;
  • has stopped eating or drinking, or hasn't slept for days;
  • may have taken too much of a medicine, alcohol or drugs.

In the US, call or text 988 (Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), or call 911. Elsewhere, call your local emergency number or crisis line. Stay with your child if you can.

2. When to talk to a professional

Not urgent, but worth an appointment soon if what you're seeing:

  • has lasted more than a couple of weeks, or keeps coming back;
  • gets in the way of school, friendships, family life, sleep or eating;
  • is getting worse, or is very different from how your child usually is;
  • leaves you unsure what's going on. Asking is always reasonable.

A good first stop is your child's doctor or pediatrician, who can check for physical causes and refer you on. The school counselor or psychologist can tell you what they see at school. A child or adolescent therapist, psychologist or psychiatrist can assess and treat. Write down what you've noticed, when it started and how often it happens; it helps any appointment.

3. What learning can and can't do

  • It can help you understand what may be going on, respond more calmly in hard moments, talk with your child in ways that keep the door open, and practice small skills together at home.
  • It can't diagnose your child or replace an assessment or treatment. It works best alongside professional help, not instead of it.
  • You're not expected to be the therapist. Your job is to be steady, connected and curious. That alone makes a real difference.

4. Common worries, and where to start in Pulse

Pulse turns parenting books into short lessons, and groups books into programs: a path through several books, step by step. These are starting points, not labels. Many children fit more than one.

  • Worry, stress, low mood or withdrawal in a teen: the Teen Stress, Anxiety, and Depression program; Helping Your Anxious Teen; The Teenage Brain.
  • Big emotions, meltdowns, intense reactions: Parenting a Child Who Has Intense Emotions; The Explosive Child; the DBT program.
  • Anxious or negative thinking patterns: the CBT program; The CBT Workbook for Teens.
  • Disorganization, forgetting, trouble starting or finishing tasks: the Executive Functioning program; Smart but Scattered (and Smart but Scattered Teens).
  • A child who thinks and learns differently (ADHD, autism): Neurodivergent – Understanding Neurodiversity.
  • Understanding how a child's brain develops: The Whole-Brain Child; Brainstorm for teens.
  • A teen who won't talk to you: How to Talk So Teens Will Listen & Listen So Teens Will Talk.
  • A grown child (19 and over) going through a hard time: they're an adult, so your role is support, not management. Start with the Staying Connected with Grown Children routines in Plan → Discover Routines, and encourage them to see a professional themselves.

5. Words you'll come across

  • Anxiety: worry or fear that's bigger or lasts longer than the situation calls for, and gets in the way of daily life.
  • Depression: low mood or loss of interest that lasts weeks, often with changes in sleep, appetite or energy. In teens it can look like irritability.
  • CBT (cognitive behavioral therapy): learning to notice unhelpful thoughts and test them, and to face worries step by step.
  • DBT (dialectical behavior therapy): skills for intense emotions: calming the body, riding out a crisis, mindfulness, and getting along with others.
  • Executive function: the brain's management skills: planning, starting tasks, remembering, switching, and controlling impulses.
  • Neurodivergent: a brain that works differently from the typical, as in ADHD or autism. Different, not broken.

6. How to start, without getting overwhelmed

  1. Pick one thing. One program, or one book. Finishing one helps more than starting five.
  2. A few minutes a day. Each lesson takes a few minutes, with a quick question at the end. Continue from Learn → My Learning.
  3. Write down what you see. Adding the hard moments as episodes, and building your child's profile, makes later suggestions fit better, and helps any appointment.
  4. Ask for suggestions. On My Learning, tap Explore books with your AI assistant (such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini). It asks a few quick questions about what you're seeing, then suggests a program or a few books. You choose what's added.
  5. Look after yourself too. Worry is exhausting. Your own calm is part of what helps your child.

This page is general information, not medical advice. It doesn't replace a professional who knows your child. If you're worried about your child's safety or someone else's, contact a professional or your local emergency services right away.
