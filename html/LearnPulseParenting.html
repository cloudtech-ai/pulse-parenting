A Guide to Pulse Parenting

Pulse Parenting helps parents raise emotionally healthy children, one small moment at a time. It brings ideas from child development, cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) and mindful parenting into a few minutes a day, and it works with the AI assistant you already use, such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini.

This page explains how Pulse works, the everyday challenges it's built around, and where to start. You can read it yourself, or ask your AI assistant to walk you through it with Pulse's Learn with button.

Start with a profile

Pulse works best once each child has a profile: a short, private picture of what's hard for them, what helps, what makes things worse, and what's going on right now. Everything else (insights, routines, weekly reviews) builds on it.

  • Where: in Pulse, open Settings → Family, tap your child, then Create Profile. (Or long-press your child's name on the Pulse tab.)
  • How: start from the blank template, or build it with your AI assistant, which asks a few questions at a time and gives back a profile for you to review.
  • Privacy: profiles stay on your phone. When you share one with your AI assistant, Pulse can replace your child's name, and you see exactly what's shared first. See the privacy policy.

The Pulse loop: Observe → Connect → Influence → Review

Pulse is built around four small habits that feed each other. You don't need to do all of them every day; a few minutes here and there, most days, is enough.

1. Observe

Notice what's really going on, without judging it yet.

  • Check-ins (Pulse tab): a quick mood and a tag or two for each family member. Over a few weeks, patterns appear, such as school days or poor sleep.
  • Episodes (Pulse tab → Add Episode on your child's card): when a hard moment happens, write a few lines about what happened. On iPhone, you can speak it instead of typing.
  • With your AI assistant: Analyze with on an episode helps you understand what may have been going on and what to try next time. It may suggest notes for your child's profile, which you choose to keep.

2. Connect

Connection comes first. Children take guidance best from someone they feel close to.

  • Connection moments (Daily Practice → Love & Connection): log the small good moments, such as a laugh, a chat in the car, or a hug at bedtime. Noticing them makes them grow.
  • Family routines: short, repeated moments together, such as a high and low at dinner, from the Family Rhythm pack.

3. Influence

Guide behavior with small, repeated routines rather than big talks in the middle of a hard moment.

  • Routines (Plan tab): find them in Discover Routines, keep the ones that fit in Your Library, and put the few you're working on now in Weekly Focus. Daily Practice shows today's.
  • Routines for you: the For You pack has two-minute resets for the parent. A steadier parent makes everything else easier.
  • With your AI assistant: Build routines with finds gaps in your library and suggests routines that fit your family. Explore with on Discover helps you learn how routines work.

4. Review

Once a week, look back at what happened and choose what to focus on next.

  • Weekly review (Review tab, or Review & plan with on the Plan tab): what went well, what was hard, and one thing learned. Then a light plan for next week, with one to three goals.
  • With your AI assistant: it reads your week (your plan, good moments, check-ins and episodes), looks back with you, then helps plan the next week. You accept what feels right.
  • What you learn feeds the next round of observing. The loop starts again, a little wiser each time.

Tour of the app

Pulse has four tabs at the bottom: Pulse, Learn, Plan and Review. Here's what each is for, how it fits the loop, and one small thing to try.

Pulse: your family today (Observe, Connect)

  • Family Pulse: a card for each family member, with Check in, Add Episode and Check-in history. Long-press a name to manage that person, including their profile. The ⚙️ next to "Family Pulse" opens Settings.
  • Today's focus: a card with today's routine (Try Now opens Daily Practice) and a Summary link to the week so far. From Friday it also reminds you about the weekly review.
  • Daily Practice: today's routines to tick off, and Record a Connection Moment for the small good moments.
  • Try this: do a quick check-in for your child: one mood and a tag or two.

Learn: understand what's going on

  • Books, videos and five-minute lessons on children's emotions, from CBT, DBT, mindful parenting and child development. Search for a topic, and pick up where you left off under Lessons in Progress.
  • Learn with your AI assistant, under "What is Pulse Parenting?", starts a conversation like this one.
  • Try this: search for a topic that matters to you right now, such as "anxiety" or "bedtime".

Plan: a light week (Influence)

  • This week: how many routines are planned, Edit plan, and Review & plan with your AI assistant.
  • Manage Routines: Build routines with your AI assistant, Discover Routines (packs for different ages, for you, and for the family), Your Library, and Weekly Focus (the few you're working on now, which a new week starts from).
  • Try this: open Discover Routines and add one routine that looks easy to start.

Review: look back (Review)

  • Weekly reviews: one line per week. Add new lets you write it yourself or do it with your AI assistant, which also plans next week.
  • Below: how moods, practice and lessons have gone over the last 7 or 30 days.
  • Try this: look at the mood chart for the last 7 days. What do you notice?

Common challenges in children's emotional health

These are general patterns, to help you understand what you may be seeing. They're not a way to diagnose your child. Every child is different, and many of these are a normal part of growing up. If you're worried, talk to your child's doctor or a mental health professional.

Worry and anxiety

  • What it can look like: avoiding things (school, new places, sleeping alone), lots of "what if" questions, stomachaches or headaches, clinginess, or anger when they're pushed toward something they fear.
  • What tends to help at home: staying calm yourself, naming the feeling ("You're worried about tomorrow"), small brave steps instead of avoiding everything, and predictable routines, especially at transitions and bedtime.
  • Talk to a professional if worry stops your child from doing everyday things, lasts for weeks, or keeps growing.

Low mood

  • What it can look like: withdrawing, losing interest in things they used to enjoy, irritability (often more than sadness, especially in teens), changes in sleep or appetite, and low energy.
  • What tends to help at home: gentle, low-pressure connection (side-by-side activities often work better than face-to-face talks), daylight and movement, small activities that bring a sense of doing something, and listening without rushing to fix.
  • Talk to a professional if low mood lasts more than two weeks, gets in the way of school or friends, or if your child talks about hopelessness or hurting themselves. In that case, seek help right away.

Big emotions and meltdowns

  • What it can look like: intense anger or tears that seem bigger than the trigger, hard transitions, and trouble calming down once upset.
  • What tends to help at home: connecting before correcting, fewer words in the moment, a calm space, naming the feeling afterwards, and practicing calming skills (like slow breathing) when everyone is calm.
  • Talk to a professional if meltdowns are frequent, dangerous, or not easing as your child grows.

Attention and focus

  • What it can look like: trouble starting or finishing tasks, forgetting steps, being easily distracted, and frustration with homework.
  • What tends to help at home: breaking tasks into one small first step, visual checklists, short work times with breaks, movement, and noticing effort rather than only results.
  • Talk to a professional if these make school or home life hard across settings and over time.

Sleep and screens

  • Why they matter: tired children have shorter fuses and more worries. Late screens push sleep later.
  • What tends to help: a regular wind-down routine, screens put away at the same time for everyone (parents too), and a calm, predictable bedtime.

Where to start this week

  1. Set up a profile for your child, if you haven't yet.
  2. Do a quick check-in on most days, and log a hard moment when one happens.
  3. Pick one or two routines for your Weekly Focus, including something small for you.
  4. At the end of the week, review it, with your AI assistant or on your own.

Privacy

Pulse keeps your family's information on your phone. When you share something with your AI assistant, you choose what goes, you see it first, and Pulse can replace your children's names. What you share goes to the AI assistant you choose, under that app's own privacy terms. Pulse doesn't keep a copy. Read the privacy policy.

Safety

Pulse and AI assistants support everyday parenting. They aren't a replacement for professional help. If you're worried about your child's safety or someone else's, contact a professional or your local emergency services right away.
